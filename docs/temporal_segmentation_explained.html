Temporal Segmentation, visually explained

The name sounds technical but the idea is simple: don't ask the GPU to render all 10 seconds at once. Render it in two 5-second chunks, then stitch the chunks so the seam is invisible. This page walks through what that means visually, why it works, and how the two chunks stay coherent.

1. The problem in one picture

Your H100 has 85 GB. A 5 s render peaks at 80.7 GB — fits, with 4 GB to spare. A 10 s render projects to ~104 GB — overflows by 19 GB. That's the OOM.

2. The core idea: split the timeline

Instead of one big tensor that holds 240 frames in GPU memory simultaneously, we make two render calls. Each call only ever holds 120 frames. The peak memory is the same as today's working 5 s — we just pay it twice.

Each segment is just a normal 5 s render that you already know works on your hardware.

3. The hard part: making the seam invisible

If you naively render two segments and concatenate them, the boundary will look like a hard cut — because segment 2's diffusion process started from random noise and has no idea what segment 1 looked like. The character could swap clothes. The background could shift. The motion direction could flip.

The fix is autoregressive conditioning with overlap:

Segment 1 frames Segment 2 frames Overlap (blended) Conditioning frames (frozen)

Why this works: by making segment 2's first 24 frames be literally the same latents as segment 1's last 24 frames, the diffusion model picks up the motion mid-stride. The character keeps walking, the camera keeps panning, the lighting stays consistent. The overlap region is rendered twice and blended — that's where any tiny mismatch dissolves.

5. Where the conditioning math comes from

Inside the diffusion loop, "conditioning" means: at every denoising step, we overwrite the first 24 positions of segment 2's latent with segment 1's tail latent (with appropriate noise added at the right sigma level). The model can't drift because those positions are pinned.

# pseudocode (matches diffusers LTXI2VLongMultiPromptPipeline)
seg1_video, seg1_tail_latents = pipeline.generate(
    prompt=prompt,
    num_frames=121,
    return_tail_latents=24,
)

# Stage 2's noise gets the tail "stamped" onto the first 24 frames at every step
seg2_video = pipeline.generate(
    prompt=prompt,                    # same prompt, or a continuation prompt
    num_frames=121,
    conditioning_latents=seg1_tail_latents,   # the anchor
    conditioning_position="head",
    overlap=24,                       # how many frames are shared
)

final = stitch(seg1_video, seg2_video, overlap=24, blend="adain")

6. Practical knobs

KnobTypical valueWhat it controls
temporal_tile_size 120 latent frames (~5 s) How many frames per segment. Cap to whatever your VRAM safely fits.
temporal_overlap 24 frames (~1 s) How many frames are shared at the boundary. Bigger = smoother seam, more recompute.
adain_factor 0.25 How aggressively to color/contrast-match segment 2 to segment 1. Hides small drift.
conditioning_position "head" Where the anchor frames go in segment 2 (always at the head, since they precede the new content).

What stays lossless

  • Frames inside each segment — bit-identical to a single render of those frames.
  • The pinned conditioning frames — they ARE segment 1's tail by construction.

What gets blended (not bit-identical)

  • The 24-frame overlap region — linear-feathered between seg1 and seg2.
  • Optional global tone match via AdaIN over the whole segment 2.

In practice this is below the visual threshold; it's the same trick used in the official LTX-2.3 long-video pipeline and ComfyUI workflows.

7. Why this is the right choice for your setup

ApproachCloses 19 GB gap?Lossless?EffortStatus
Temporal segmentation yes (~24 GB) inside segments Medium Proven in 3+ public workflows
FFN forward chunking partial (2–4 GB) bit-identical Medium Useful complement, not enough alone
FP8 native matmul ~25 GB resident numerical High (base image swap) Upstream issue still open
Reduce resolution yes no — quality drop Low Not what you want

8. The one-line summary

Temporal segmentation = render two 5-second clips back-to-back, where the second one starts from the last second of the first one. Stitch with a short overlap. The GPU never has to hold all 10 seconds in memory at the same time.
